# Six reflections

*Todd Youngblood, Ventures and Operations Steward, RegenHub, LCA. Dictated 2026-07-27, eighteen days before the launch gathering. Transcribed and lightly edited: dictation artifacts repaired, phrasing kept. Drafts, open to correction.*

---

## I. The work as craft, not commerce

*1:42 PM*

I keep returning to what Techne could be as a soil and a substrate: for art, for craft, and for science.

What makes it distinct is not its legal form alone but what that form protects. A corporation treats the work, the craft, and the outputs of labor as merely commerce. In doing so it debases the work. It takes labor from an elevated form and returns it in a base one, and it does this not out of malice but because commerce is the only register it has.

I think this container is unusual. Most incubators and accelerators for founders treat a founder's work as an opportunity for commerce and profit, and in treating it that way they define the end goal and the north star of that work. Everything upstream bends toward the exit. The work becomes the means; the return becomes the meaning.

Craft inverts that. Craft returns what some might call a sacred relationship to the work: the relationship that lets a thing become what it could and should be, at its highest purpose rather than its lowest. Commerce, profit, and gain are real, and a cooperative that cannot meet its lease does not survive to protect anything. But they are the base purpose, not the high one, and I want to be honest about which is which.

The question I am holding is whether a cooperative can hold that distinction structurally rather than aspirationally. Not a values statement about craft, but a form in which the craft is what the accounting actually recognizes.

## II. Kairos

*1:51 PM*

The Greeks had more than one word for time. The one I keep reaching for is *kairos*: not duration, but the moment that asks for the right action.

I think we are in one. In 2026, and particularly in the state of AI in July of 2026, knowledge workers and the entire sector of the economy that depends on their knowledge and intellectual capacity for labor find themselves at risk. That is the plain fact, and I do not want to soften it.

But this moment also offers what very few populations are offered in their own time: a fundamental shift that could enable the cooperative and common control and operation of the means of production. The same technology that puts knowledge work at risk is the technology that makes a transition possible, one in which digital infrastructure becomes a public good. It could balance the current over-concentration of privately controlled platforms and offer a more plural ecosystem, one that does not lock in the participants and guests of the World Wide Web.

So I read this point in time as kairos, as a moment speaking to us. It says: embrace the means of production. Take up the work ahead of us, which is the work of governing, and collectively benefiting from, a commons of digital infrastructure, held by a decentralized network of cooperatives organized and self-organized according to art, craft, and science, and to the fields where those intersect.

Moments like this do not stay open. That is what makes them kairos rather than chronos.

## III. Who a third space is for

*2:04 PM*

This follows from the first reflection and may complete it.

The corporate form cannot honor work as craft. I think it also cannot honor the worker as a craftsperson, an artist, or a scientist, depending on the field. Not because the people inside it lack the wish to, but because the form has no place to record it.

That absence is what casts Techne as a possible third space, distinct from traditional corporate work, where a number of populations and identities could find meaningful engagement.

I picture four people. A student who wants to practice, or simply to play with new ideas. Someone between jobs who wants to stay relevant, to learn, or to teach and help educate. A mid-career professional who was laid off and is seeking to transition to another kind of work, but who is not yet contemplating retirement. And someone at retirement age, for whom this could be a meaningful interface to pass down their practice, their wisdom, and their inherited knowledge, through the distinct lens of their identity, their generation, and their history.

None of those four is well served by a firm. All four have something to give and something to gain from a place organized around practice rather than employment. If Techne is a soil, these are the people who tend it, and the question of ownership of work is really the question of whether the record can hold what each of them brings.

## IV. Why Colorado

*3:18 PM*

A consideration of place, and of why the birthplace is not incidental.

Colorado is a legislative and policy frontier among the United States, specifically in economic and cooperative law. It is not alone in this. It has siblings: Wyoming in the law of decentralized autonomous organizations, South Dakota in trust law. States that decided, deliberately, to be the place where a new kind of entity could first be legal. Colorado made that decision about cooperatives, and that is what makes it a distinctive birthplace for Techne.

What the Limited Cooperative Association gives us specifically is a container that can hold a right relationship with investor members. Not a form that refuses capital, and not a form that is captured by it. Our bylaws are precise about the terms of that relationship: an Investor Member is a non-voting member who contributes capital, and does not have voting rights unless they also qualify as a patron member in another class. Capital is admitted as a member and given a real stake. It is not given a voice, unless it takes up the obligations of patronage alongside everyone else, in which case the voice it gains is the patron's, not the capital's.

That is the whole proposition, and it is why the container matters as much as the intention: it lets Techne be a meaningful commons that can partner with capital without becoming dependent on it. Most forms make you choose between refusing capital and being steered by it. This one lets us hold both, and then writes down, in the bylaws rather than in our values, which one governs.

## V. The scope, and what it became

*3:26 PM, February's documents, May's feedback, August's system*

In February, Kevin and I wrote down what this role was for. The Statement of Work put it in three lines: *Focus: Ventures and Operations. Signal: Legibility. Measure: The Revenue Bottom Line.* The job description put it in one: make ventures legible to capital, financial and otherwise. Three scopes followed. A venture studio on the third floor. The acquisition of 1515 Walnut. And a capital interface, where I would author what the document called translator artifacts, the models and plans and decks that let external evaluators understand where to send resources.

In May I brought both documents to the cooperative and asked a question I meant literally: how may I best serve you, as members of the co-op. That is the moment the role changed hands. It had been a scope initiated under a sponsor. It became a mandate answerable to a board, and Kevin said so himself in the thread, in the plainest possible terms: push back, I am but one of eight.

The answers narrowed me, and I needed narrowing. Own the financials and make them comprehensible, on a schedule. Treat revenue as something I support by shaping the financial layer rather than something I carry alone. Build an accountability structure for myself that works for the cooperative without becoming overhead. Face the question of continuity when the six-month sponsorship ends. And from Aaron, the request that has shaped more of my year than anyone intended: clarify, in simple and understandable terms, what it actually means to be a member, an investor member, a contributing member. There was also a caution I took seriously, that the fantasies and longshots should be unprioritized where they detract from what generates revenue soon.

Then we lost the third floor to a competitive lease offer, and two of February's three scopes went with it. Against the ninety and hundred-eighty day milestones we wrote, that is a shortfall, and I would rather say so than dress it.

What I have built answers May's brief more closely than February's. Aaron asked for the member classes to be intelligible; the bylaws now say in four plain entries what each class is, an investor member is a non-voting member who contributes capital unless they also hold patronage, and a member can read the agreement that binds them without asking anyone. Kevin asked for an accountability structure that works without overhead; what emerged is a public ledger of the work itself, where every packet names what it delivers, what blocks it, and who must act, and where nothing may display as settled before it is. I did not set out to answer that request with a ledger. It is, on reflection, the only honest form the request could take.

The financial half is where I must be exact. The outcome named first, comprehensible financials arriving on a schedule, is the outcome the treasury module is designed to serve, and the treasury module is specified and sequenced rather than built. That ordering is deliberate: money moves last, into a record already proven to reconcile. It is also, plainly, the part of the mandate that is least finished.

So the three emphases stand where they stand. Legibility of the entity is delivered. Recognition of the work completed thus far is built and waiting on the board to adopt the rules that would compute it. Access to capital is specified and sequenced last. And the sponsorship that started this concludes at roughly the moment the cooperative launches, which makes the fourth thing Kevin asked for, continuity, a live question rather than a distant one.

There is a personal edge to the middle one that I will name rather than hide. The part of the system that would recognize contribution, including my own, is precisely the part still waiting on a board act. That is the right order. A cooperative that computed its steward's recognition before adopting the rules that govern it would have proven exactly the wrong thing about itself.

## VI. What computing was for

*4:17 PM*

This is the consideration I have held longest, most of a year now, and it is the one underneath all the others.

Computing and information technology were conceived as a tool for humanity to address its most pressing challenges collectively: through the sharing of knowledge and information, and through the building of systems. That was the founding intention of the field, and it is worth saying that it was an intention, held by people, and not an inevitability.

What happened instead is drift, and then capture. Corporate structures took up these tools and pointed them at profit maximization, and in doing so the field lost its soul. I do not think that word is too strong. A tool imagined for collective coordination became an instrument of extraction and enclosure, and we have spent a first century of computing living inside the consequences of that turn.

Our own formation conversation reached this in January, days before we filed. The observation in the room was that the first century of computing was shaped by the corporate co-option of tools first imagined for collective coordination, and that alongside a housing right there is an information right. We filed a week later. I have not stopped thinking that the two facts belong together.

What is different now is that in the era of AI and collective intelligence we can actually build the thing. Real cybernetic systems, and real cybernetic organizations: entities that sense their own condition, that respond, that are accountable to the people inside them. Organizations in a position to transform corporate structures into responsive human entities, capable of addressing systemic risks and harms rather than generating them.

This is why the first principle of the system I am presenting is that it exists to augment human coordination and not to replace human judgment. Agents draft, surface, and guide; people decide, sign, and govern. That is not a safety disclaimer. It is the whole argument of this reflection, written where it has to be written to mean anything, which is into the design.

And it suggests a different theory of how change happens. Not systemically forced, imposed from above on populations who did not ask for it, but locally organic: grown in particular places by particular people who can see what they are participating in. A cooperative in Boulder is a small instance of that. It is also, as far as I can tell, the only kind that has ever worked.
